Michelin — modernizing 20-year-old software

A Tanzu Labs engagement with Michelin — turning a vague "clean up the UI" request into a validated product direction, shipping in 12 weeks, and building a user-centered design practice from scratch.

Role
Product designer & design lead — Tanzu Labs consultant
Context
Enterprise B2B · tire retreading software
Focus
Discovery → research → prototype → ship + mentorship
Timeline
Spring 2023 · shipped in 12 weeks

The situation

Michelin came to Tanzu Labs for help with three things: re-platforming their tire retreading software (BibTread), solving user pains that were causing customer attrition, and learning Lean, Agile, and user-centered design methodologies.

The challenge wasn't just the software. The client team trusted gut over data and had a habit of prioritizing features without validation. My job was to run a user-centered process in front of them — and let the results make the case.

The problem as stated — and the real one

Client's stated ask

"The existing customer data entry UI needs to be cleaned up."

What research revealed

Too many manual steps to get data from salespeople — causing incomplete, error-prone records and a screenshots-based sharing process.

The gap between these two framings was the whole engagement. Getting the team to see and trust that gap is what made the rest possible.

How I worked

Five phases, one consistent thread: make the real problem visible before committing to a solution, then ship with data to prove it worked.

01

Discover & research

Service blueprint to surface pains, 2x2 prioritization, then a full-team user research round to validate the top assumption.

02

Design & prototype

Design studio to align on a lean solution, prototype in Michelin's design system, user-tested with the pair running the sessions.

03

Build & measure

Strangler-pattern delivery to ship alongside the old system, instrumented with analytics and an explicit adoption target.

Discovery

I ran a service blueprint to map user pains end-to-end, then wrote each pain as a testable assumption. I facilitated a 2x2 — high potential impact on users, low confidence in a solution. Two goals: start the roadmap where it would help most, and show the client what structured thinking looks like.

Prioritized assumption

Administrators manually enter lots of customer data to process tires, and it is rife with errors that damage customer relationships.

high impact · low confidence
2x2 prioritization — impact on users vs. confidence in a solution

Research

I led the full team — including external stakeholders — through user research. After interviews and synthesis, we refined the assumption into a validated problem.

The real win was what it did to the room: the client team saw first-hand how little they knew about actual user behavior, and what that gap was costing them.

"User research is a game changer for us."

Client PM

Refined user problem

There are too many manual steps to get data from salespeople — causing incomplete and error-prone customer records. Administrators are frequently asked to send customer records to salespeople, a manual process involving screenshots.

Validated in user research · previously framed as a UI cosmetic issue

Design studio

With a validated problem, I ran a design studio with the full team. Rapid ideation, diverge then converge — and we aligned on a lean solution in a single session.

The lean solution
Administrator enters customer data on one screen — not multiple — matching how they receive the data
Form fields with inline validation to catch errors at entry, before they damage records
"Export to PDF" for easy sharing with salespeople — replacing the screenshots workflow entirely
Design studio output — rapid ideation, aligned in one session

Prototype & validate

My pair and I built a prototype using Michelin's existing design system, then returned to users for another round of testing. This time, I had my pair run their very first user testing session.

Users responded immediately to the single-screen layout and the PDF export — exactly what they'd wanted without knowing how to ask.

"I never knew it could look this simple."

User testing participant

With validated designs and a field-mapping diagram showing how database tables would consolidate, we moved to build.

Build & measure

New software was in users' hands 12 weeks after kickoff. The team used the Strangler pattern — keeping the existing application operational while introducing new features, then directing users to the new surface via links in key places.

We instrumented the new features with analytics and set an explicit adoption goal. We thought it was ambitious. We were right to be ambitious.

The goal

20 users

unique "Export" clicks in 30 days

The result

36 users

engaged in the first week

Next iteration

We moved on to the next problem: putting customer data entry in the hands of salespeople. The risk was immediate — no one on the team had ever spoken to salespeople. Would they be willing to take on data entry at all?

We validated the assumption. Salespeople weren't just willing — they were enthusiastic. No one on the client team had considered it possible. It opened a path to eliminating the administrative process and giving salespeople ownership of their own records.

"This is way better than what we have now."

Salesperson — user testing

Mentorship

Alongside everything above, I was teaching my pair how to be a product designer from scratch — Figma, user research, lean process. He started by observing. By the end, he was driving our pairing sessions.

Months later I caught up with him: he was driving responsive design improvements on his own. His growth is what I'm proudest of from this engagement — more than any feature or metric.

Outcomes

Shipped in 12 weeks

New software in users' hands from kickoff to launch, on an aggressive timeline with a new team.

36 users in week one

Exceeded the 30-day adoption goal of 20 users within the first week after release.

Monthly → 2× weekly

Release cadence improved from monthly to twice weekly during the engagement.

A practice built

Client team ended the engagement focused on user value, evangelizing UCD to the rest of Michelin.

This project was a reminder of the power of user-centered design. Often, clients aren't aware of the "art of the possible." By the end, clients and stakeholders were focused on high-value user experiences over useless features — and carrying these ideas back to the rest of their organization. That mindset shift was, to me, more valuable than any single feature we shipped.

What it taught me

The most valuable thing you can give a client isn't a feature — it's a way of seeing.

When a team starts trusting research over assumption, the roadmap changes permanently. Getting the team to that shift was the real deliverable.
