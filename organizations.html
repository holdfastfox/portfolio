Organizations — the scoping model that runs the platform

Designing the selector and scoping model that decides what every user sees — and making it hold up as organizations grow into deep, messy hierarchies.

Role
Product designer — interaction design, shipped UI
Context
Enterprise developer-tools platform
Focus
Model → resilient states → shipped UI
Collaboration
Engineering, design system

The problem

The organization selector is the lens the entire platform is seen through — it scopes data, permissions, and navigation. It's also tiny and easy to underestimate. As customers grew, their hierarchies got deep and their names got long, and the original selector started to buckle on exactly the cases that matter most: slow loads, failures, deep nesting, and "wait, which org am I even in?"

Constraints

  • It governs everything. A bug here doesn't break one screen — it scopes the whole product wrong.
  • Hierarchies get deep. The model had to stay legible as organizations nested several levels down.
  • Resilient by default. Loading, error, and empty states couldn't be afterthoughts.
  • Small surface, big stakes. Every pixel of a control this central is load-bearing.

How I worked

01

Map the model

Traced how scoping flows through navigation, data, and permissions so the UI matched the real mental model.

02

Design the edges

Treated loading, error, empty, long-name, and deep-nesting as first-class states, not exceptions.

03

Ship

Delivered the selector plus a dedicated management page, in step with engineering.

Designing the edges, not just the happy path

The interesting design work in a selector like this isn't the default — it's everything that happens when the data is slow, missing, deeply nested, or named something forty characters long. Designing those states deliberately is what made the control feel trustworthy.

Key decisions & tradeoffs

  • States as first-class. Designing loading, error, and empty up front cost time but removed whole classes of confusion later.
  • A dedicated management page. Rather than cram administration into the picker, I split it out — the picker stays fast, management gets room.
  • Graceful truncation. Long names get handled predictably instead of breaking the layout.

Outcomes

A resilient selector

Shipped a selector that holds up under deep hierarchies and the messy real-world edge cases.

Room to administer

A dedicated management page gave organization administration its own clear home.

Less "where am I?"

Clearer scoping reduced confusion about what a user was actually looking at.

What it taught me

A control this small touches everything — the edge cases are the design.
