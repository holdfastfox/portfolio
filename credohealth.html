The product

Credo Health built PreDx — a platform that helps healthcare organizations with risk adjustment coding. In short: when a patient has documented medical conditions, those diagnoses need to be captured accurately for insurance reimbursement. If they're missed, the organization loses revenue and patients may receive less appropriate care.

PreDx uses machine learning to read patient medical records — PDFs of charts, discharge summaries, lab results, consult notes — and surface potential ICD-10 diagnosis codes with supporting evidence. Clinicians and risk adjusters then review, validate, and complete the audit. The product reduces the manual work of reading thousands of pages per patient while keeping clinicians in control of every decision.

The design challenge

The core tension: AI suggestions are only useful if clinicians trust them. In a clinical context, a confident-but-wrong code suggestion isn't just unhelpful — it can cause real harm. Every design decision about how suggestions were surfaced, what evidence was shown, and how feedback was collected had to work for skeptical, expert users who would not tolerate a system that felt like a black box.

  • Trust and evidence. Every suggested code needed to link directly to the supporting evidence in the source document — the specific page, the specific section.
  • Clinician control. Accept, edit, or reject — always. The AI accelerates; the clinician decides.
  • Speed at scale. Risk adjusters process large volumes of charts. The interface had to support efficient review without cutting corners on accuracy.
  • No prior design system. When I joined, there were no reusable components, no shared styles, no documented patterns.

Building the design system

When I joined there was no shared component library. I built the Credo Design Library from the ground up — colors, typography, spacing tokens, and a full component set: buttons, text fields, tooltips, chips, dropdowns, evidence placeholders, and icons.

The evidence placeholder component was specific to the product: a pattern for surfacing a snippet of source document (a page screenshot, a highlighted section) alongside a diagnosis code suggestion. Getting that component right meant thinking through the AI-to-clinician handoff at the component level, not just the page level.

The chart audit flow

The core product experience: a risk adjuster or clinician opens a patient, sees their chart files (organized by type, specialty, location, and date), and works through the audit. The AI surfaces potential ICD-10 codes in a sidebar, each linked to the specific evidence page in the document.

Key decisions in this flow: sticky sidebar header/footer so actions are always reachable; file sorted by type, specialty, location, and date so auditors could navigate without reading every document; page count instead of file size because that's what matters when you're deciding which documents to open first.

The AI feedback loop

One of the highest-stakes features I designed was the ML document summary — an AI-generated plain-language summary of each patient chart document, surfaced as a beta feature alongside the raw document. The goal: help clinicians orient faster so they could spend their attention on the complex judgment calls, not on decoding a 25-page discharge summary.

The trust problem was real. In a clinical context, an AI summary that gets something wrong and isn't caught could be worse than no summary. I designed the feature with explicit uncertainty signals — a "BETA" label, a clear "AI generated summary" attribution, and a structured feedback mechanism (thumbs up/down) to let clinicians flag summaries that felt inaccurate.

The feedback data fed back to the ML team to improve model quality — a loop where the clinicians using the product were actively shaping the AI behind it. Getting clinicians to engage with that feedback required the design to make it feel low-effort and meaningful, not just a checkbox.

What I shipped

A partial list of features designed and delivered across the engagement:

  • Chart audit flow. Patient list, file viewer, ICD-10 code panel with evidence linking, audit start/finish.
  • ML document summary. AI-generated summaries with structured clinician feedback (thumbs up/down).
  • Internal document search. Keyword search within patient charts, with empty states and loading patterns.
  • FHIR data tabs. Medications, labs, and vitals tabs populated from structured FHIR data alongside uploaded documents.
  • Patient request flow (PreHx). Form for requesting new patient records — with address, NPI, encounter type/location, org selection, and validation states.
  • Account creation & authentication. Sign-up, login, forgot password, Doximity SSO, and click-wrap flows.
  • Credo Design Library. Full component set built from scratch and maintained throughout the engagement.

What it taught me

In a clinical AI product, trust isn't a feature — it's the foundation everything else sits on.

Transparency about what the AI is doing, explicit uncertainty markers, and giving experts a clear path to override aren't constraints on the product — they're what makes the product usable. Designing that feedback loop well meant the AI could get better without asking clinicians to trust something they couldn't see.
