Trigrep: code search, tested from first principles

Six enterprise engineering interviews reversed the core assumption: natural-language-first wasn't the answer. Here's what the research found, and what shipped instead.

Role
Lead product designer: research, interaction design, prototyping
Context
Enterprise developer-tools platform
Focus
Research → interaction model → shipped UI
Collaboration
Engineering, product, design system

The problem

The search engine was powerful, but wielding it meant knowing exact syntax, scopes, and filters. Users had no on-ramp: no syntax examples in-tool, no docs link surfaced at the point of need. The team assumed a natural-language AI assistant would solve this. Research said otherwise.

Constraints

  • Trust and accuracy. In an enterprise engineering context, a confident-but-wrong answer is worse than no answer.
  • Explainability. Results had to show why they matched, not just that they matched.
  • Fit the existing workflow. The assistant couldn't be a walled garden; it needed to connect to full-power search.
  • Speed expectations. Engineers expect search to feel instant.

At a glance

I logged explicit assumptions first, then tested them before any UI was built. The main assumption: natural language was the right entry point.

01

Research

Six interviews across three enterprise engineering organizations. Two rounds: one to test the AI-first hypothesis, one to test the revised direction.

02

Pivot

Research reversed the core architecture decision. What started as AI-first split into two tracks: the query interface shipped first; the AI assistant became a parallel path.

03

Design & ship

Shipped the query interface with the critical path built in: search → export to CSV → execute a transformation. Users had no on-ramp to the query syntax; inline hints and examples provided one.

The initial hypothesis

The first prototype led with natural language: ask in plain language, get ranked results, open full search to refine. This is what we took into research.

Illustrative: recreated for portfolio; not the production UI

Key decisions & tradeoffs

  • Two separate tracks, not one combined surface. Some users wanted natural-language search. Others wanted direct query control. One interface couldn't serve both. We shipped the query interface first; the AI assistant became a parallel path.
  • Export and execute as primary actions. Search is never the endpoint. The path: search → export → align → execute a transformation. Both actions had to be prominent in the results view, not buried.
  • Inline syntax hints over AI translation. One engineer gave up on their first attempt: no examples, no in-tool guidance. Instead of replacing syntax with natural language, we made it learnable in context: a query-language selector with inline examples.
  • Warm, slightly stale index over rebuild-on-demand. Engineers tolerated 30–60 minute index freshness. We prioritized warm sync. The real investment: making empty-vs-error states clear so a slow search never looks like a failure.

Outcomes

Core assumption reversed

Six interviews across three enterprise engineering organizations disproved the AI-first hypothesis before any production work began.

Shipped the right thing

The query-based interface shipped first with export and execute as primary actions: the workflow the research said mattered most.

Architecture decision changed

Research moved AI search and query search from one combined surface to two separate tracks: a product-level decision, not just a UI one.

What it taught me

Research that tells you you're wrong early is the most useful kind.

The team went in assuming natural language was the answer. Six interviews said otherwise. The biggest output wasn't a design direction. It was an architecture decision.
