Trigrep — natural-language code search

An AI assistant as the front door to a powerful code-search engine — so engineers could ask for what they wanted in plain language instead of memorizing query syntax.

Role
Lead product designer — research, interaction design, prototyping
Context
Enterprise developer-tools platform
Focus

The problem

The search engine underneath was genuinely powerful — but wielding it meant knowing exact syntax, scopes, and filters. That put the most capable tool in the platform out of reach for the people who needed it most in the moment. The opportunity: let an assistant translate plain-language intent into a precise, trustworthy search.

Constraints

  • Trust and accuracy. In an enterprise engineering context, a confident-but-wrong answer is worse than no answer.
  • Explainability. Results had to show why they matched, not just that they matched.
  • Fit the existing workflow. The assistant couldn't be a walled garden — it needed a path into the full-power search surface.
  • Speed expectations. Engineers expect search to feel instant.

How I worked

I started from explicit assumptions, tested them with real engineers, and let what I heard reshape the interaction model before any UI was finalized.

01

Research

Logged assumptions, then ran two rounds of interviews with engineers — including at a large enterprise customer — to learn how people actually phrase intent.

02

Interaction model

Designed the conversational entry point and a results surface that stays explainable, with a path into full-power search.

03

Prototype & ship

Iterated a working prototype against feedback, then partnered with engineering to ship the assistant's component suite.

The interaction model

Two surfaces, one path between them: a conversational assistant for intent, and a traditional split-panel search for power and precision. You can start by asking, then "open in search" to refine with filters — or skip straight to the power tool.

Key decisions & tradeoffs

  • Assistant and power tool, not either/or. Rather than replace search with chat, I connected them — intent up front, precision on demand.
  • Explainability over magic. Showing why a result matched cost some visual simplicity but bought the trust the context demanded.
  • Progressive disclosure. Start simple, reveal filters and scope only when someone reaches for them.

Outcomes

A validated model

Two research rounds turned assumptions into an interaction model the team had confidence to build.

Shipped to production

The work shipped as a reusable component suite for the assistant.

Drove decisions

Research directly shaped engineering and product direction beyond the UI itself.

What it taught me

The fastest way to earn trust in an AI feature isn't to hide the machinery — it's to show the working.

Plain-language input is only half the job. The other half is making the system's reasoning legible enough that an expert will actually rely on it.
